02
Gap one · The redesign

The rebuild was not a rescue.
It was a redesign.

When the development team went offline in late 2025, the instinct most founders would follow is replacement — find another developer, pick up where the last team left off, finish what was started.

That is not what happened.

The original architecture was a proper microservices stack. ASP.NET Core 9, PostgreSQL, RabbitMQ, Vue.js dashboards. Thirty-six normalized tables. Multiple decoupled services communicating across boundaries. Technically competent. Well-designed for a human engineering team.

The problem is that microservices are designed by and for human brains. They require developers who can hold the entire mental model in their heads, navigate between services on demand, manage distributed tracing, maintain interface segregations, and debug across boundaries. When the human team disappeared, the architecture became a liability — not because it was broken, but because it was the wrong structure for what came next.

The decision that mattered was not "how do we fix this codebase." It was "what kind of codebase can be operated without a permanent human engineering team."

The answer was fat controllers.

Traditional software engineering treats fat controllers as an anti-pattern — a sign of poor design, too much logic concentrated in one place, hard to maintain by human developers. That critique is correct when the developer is human. It inverts completely when the developer is an AI agent.

AI agents operate with high confidence when context is consolidated and deterministic. They lose coherence when they have to maintain mental state across distributed service boundaries. A fat controller — a single, comprehensive execution scope containing a complete workflow path — is the natural interface for an AI agent. It can read the entire context, modify it, test it, and ship it without losing track of what it was doing.

The architectural pivot was deliberate. The microservices boundaries were consolidated. The two Vue.js 2.6 dashboard applications were decommissioned and replaced with a unified React 18 / TypeScript / Vite frontend. The fragmented API response structures were replaced with a single mandatory envelope — every endpoint returns the same wrapper, every time, no exceptions. The permission lookup tables were flattened. The patterns that made the system navigable for human developers were replaced with patterns that made it operable for AI agents.

Then the rebuild happened. Twenty-seven days. Zero critical defects in production.

This is not a story about AI finishing what humans started. It is a story about understanding what AI needs in order to operate independently, and redesigning the system to provide it.

03
Gap two · The philosophy

The onboarding was not a feature.
It was a philosophy.

Once the platform was rebuilt and running, the next problem was partner activation.

The standard solution to slow partner onboarding is better documentation. Cleaner guides, more detailed API references, longer getting-started tutorials, better tooltips. Product teams have been iterating on this approach for twenty years. It is the wrong answer to the right problem.

Documentation assumes the partner will read it, understand it, correctly apply it, and stay patient while they figure out what went wrong. Most partners do not. They are busy. They have internal stakeholders to convince. They need to see the product work before they can sell it internally. A document does not give them that. A document asks them to trust before they have seen anything worth trusting.

The question we asked was different. Not "how do we make the documentation clearer" but "what if we eliminated the need for documentation entirely."

The result was what we called the 60-Second Path — a four-step sequence from signup to first successful API transaction, designed so that a partner could prove the system works before they had to understand how it works.

The three most common failure modes — invalid API key, unfunded wallet, empty catalog — surface immediately with clear, actionable messages rather than returning cryptic errors that take hours to diagnose. We built the failure paths as carefully as the success path, because great onboarding is fast diagnosis, not just happy paths.

"The barrier to a signed partnership is rarely the commercial terms. It is the internal conviction that the thing actually works."
Fifteen years of deals stalling — Sudan, Abu Dhabi, Dubai

The philosophy behind this came from fifteen years of watching deals stall. In Sudan, in Abu Dhabi, in Dubai, the pattern was consistent: the barrier to a signed partnership is rarely the commercial terms. It is the internal conviction that the thing actually works. You cannot convince someone of that with a document. You can convince them by doing the first part of the work for them and letting them see the result.

That insight — eliminate the work rather than explain it, make the partner a reviewer rather than a builder — is what the onboarding was designed to embody.

The original article described the platform as "live and stress tested." That is true and insufficient. The more important thing is that a partner can go from first contact to first successful API transaction in under sixty seconds, with zero documentation, and walk away with proof rather than a promise.

04
Gap three · How I work

Not a developer.
A precise description of the gap.

The two stories above — the architectural redesign and the onboarding philosophy — are not just Steller stories. They are the clearest examples I have of how I actually think and operate.

I am not a developer. I cannot write the code. What I can do is understand what a system needs to accomplish, identify the structural conditions that would allow it to accomplish that, and work backward from the outcome to the design. That is what the fat controller pivot was. That is what the 60-Second Path was. Neither required me to write a line of code. Both required a clear answer to the question: what does this need to be, and why does the current version fail to be it.

Across every role I have held, the consistent pattern is the same. Find the gap between what exists and what should exist. Understand it precisely enough to describe the solution. Build or commission what is needed to close it. Move on to the next gap.

The Steller story is the fullest expression of that pattern. The gap was real — B2B companies had no clean infrastructure for digital gift card programs. The solution required an architectural judgment call under pressure — microservices to fat controllers — that most people in my position would not have made because most people in my position would not have understood why it mattered. The execution required seven-hour sessions on a picnic chair in a cold basement, directing AI agents through a rebuild without being able to read the code they were writing.

And the result is a live platform, stress tested to zero critical defects, currently running an active API integration with Bede — the digital wallet of Zain Sudan, one of Africa's largest telecoms — and onboarding partners to their first successful API transaction in under sixty seconds.

That is the story the original article did not tell.
